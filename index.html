Matthew Hutson

Journalist / Cognitive scientist / Writer

Matthew Hutson (MH) is a science journalist based in New York. He studied cognitive neuroscience at Brown University and science writing at MIT. He was news editor at Psychology Today from 2006 to 2010 and later wrote for outlets including The New Yorker, Wired, The Atlantic and Nature. He wrote The 7 Laws of Magical Thinking (2012), about how irrational beliefs can keep us happy, healthy and sane.

When the lowest point has a purpose

Adapted from published writing and research by Matthew Hutson. Not a live interview.

MHI had a tough time in high school. I felt isolated and fundamentally flawed, and I stopped getting out of bed. Eventually people around me stepped in and I was hospitalised. It scared me straight, because it showed me a path of suffering I no longer wanted. I went back on medication and did what it took to stay at my school.

MHAbout one in six Americans will have major depression at some point. The word “disorder” makes us see it as a breakdown, a flaw in the system to be fixed and moved past. Some psychologists argue it isn’t a dysfunction at all, but an evolved mechanism built to achieve particular benefits. If they’re right, our thinking about depression needs an intervention too.

MHIn one study of 61 depressed people, four out of five reported at least one upside to rumination, like self-insight, problem solving and avoiding future mistakes. Most episodes also end on their own, which is called spontaneous remission. Steven Hollon thinks the rumination idea may explain this. He adds that cognitive behavioural and problem-solving therapies may work because they speed up, to weeks, a process that evolved to take months.

MHA small minority of researchers, like Edward Hagen, think we may have evolved to attempt suicide under the right conditions. Inclusive fitness says your genes may lead you to sacrifice yourself if it benefits relatives. The bargaining model relies on “costly signalling”: a peacock’s tail honestly signals fitness, and a hungry chick’s loud chirp signals need. Here, an attempt signals a need for help, and the vast majority of attempts are not fatal.

MHHagen’s team analysed 474 records from 53 cultures. One in three described the victim as a burden, but many more showed family members who were worse off. Attempts were often public, non-fatal and rewarded with help, and victims were often powerless and in conflict with others. In a 1958 Papua New Guinea case, a girl’s repeated attempts finally won her the marriage she wanted. Studies of social support fit too. Matthew Nock calls the models compelling, though he says the evidence isn’t a clincher.

MHHagen and Tom Rosenström analysed 4,192 American adults. Their idea is that men, being physically stronger, use anger to bargain, while women rely more on depression. People with greater upper body strength were less likely to be depressed, and once strength was removed, men and women were equally likely to be depressed. Hagen also sees postpartum depression as costly signalling, where a mother’s lost interest recruits help from a partner or community.

MHUp to 80 percent of depression follows major life events. A close relative’s death raised a woman’s risk 20 times in the following month. The fight is over the other 20 percent, which have no obvious trigger. Thomas Joiner says triggers, like memories, are usually there. But you can always find something in someone’s past, so the idea that depression is a fitting response to stress is nearly impossible to test.

MHI sometimes lament how much more industrious I’d be without my depression, which is now manageable. But I allow that even today my melancholia may have benefits. It focuses me on deeper questions about where I’m going in life, by making me question the value of everything, including depression itself.

Fisher

Helen Fisher

Anthropologist / Researcher / Author

Helen Fisher (HF) (1945–2024) was a biological anthropologist and a senior research fellow at the Kinsey Institute. She held a PhD from the University of Colorado Boulder and was a member of Rutgers University’s Center for Human Evolutionary Studies. She co-authored some of the first fMRI studies of romantic love and wrote Anatomy of Love, The First Sex, Why We Love and Why Him? Why Her? From 2005 she advised Match.com, created the Fisher Temperament Inventory and ran the Singles in America survey with the Kinsey Institute. Her TED talks on love have drawn over 20 million views.

Love as a natural addiction

Adapted from published writing and research by Helen Fisher. Not a live interview.

HFI maintain that romantic love is an addiction: wonderful when it goes well, horribly negative when it breaks down. Even happy lovers show an addict’s traits: craving, intoxication, intensification, obsessive thinking and separation anxiety. Rejection looks like drug withdrawal, with crying, lethargy, changes in sleep and appetite, irritability and loneliness. Lovers even relapse, when songs, places or people tied to the ex trigger craving and compulsive calling or showing up.

HFIn Love and Limerence, Dorothy Tennov named this state “limerence.” It starts with salience, then intrusive thinking that can fill 85 to 100 percent of your day. Crystallisation makes you magnify the person’s traits and excuse their flaws. Craving, hope and uncertainty dominate, and obstacles intensify the passion, which I call “frustration attraction.” Underneath is fear, with trembling, a pounding heart, trouble eating or sleeping, and above all a feeling of helplessness.

HFInfatuated lovers want sex, but the craving for emotional union overshadows it. 95 percent of Tennov’s women and 91 percent of her men rejected the idea that the best thing about love is sex. Age matters little too: in my questionnaire of 437 Americans and 402 Japanese, people over 45 and under 25 didn’t differ significantly on 82 percent of the questions. Love usually first appears around puberty, but I met a two-and-a-half-year-old boy who stroked a girl’s hair and was depressed for two hours after she left.

HFMany assume all addictions are harmful, but Bartels and Zeki found that happy lovers and euphoric cocaine or opioid users share reward-system regions. In 1996 I began planning an fMRI study where lovers would look at a photo of their beloved and a neutral photo, counting backward by sevens in between. I expected dopamine to dominate, since it drives energy, craving and focus. I thought norepinephrine might add focus and bodily symptoms, and low serotonin might explain the obsessive thinking.

HFWe scanned 17 people who were happily in love and saw activity in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), a dopamine factory in the reward system. Lovers are high on natural “speed.” Men showed as much VTA activity as women, and Chinese participants matched too, so almost everyone feels this passion. I came to see love as a basic drive. Baseline dopamine varies between people, and rare conditions like hypopituitarism can cause “love blindness.”

HFWe scanned 15 people dumped about 63 days earlier, who spent over 85 percent of their waking hours thinking of the ex. Reward regions lit up, including areas tied to attachment, physical pain and the craving seen in cocaine addicts. They were still deeply in love, and in pain. In one college community, 93 percent had been spurned and 95 percent had rejected someone. First comes protest, with obsessive efforts to win the person back, then despair as hope fades.

HFRejection can trigger “abandonment rage,” as prefrontal reward regions stimulate the amygdala when an expected reward is lost, stressing the heart and weakening immunity. This protest response is mammalian: puppies and baby rats cry out when separated. Combined with craving and stress, it plays a role in crimes of passion. When protest fails, despair follows. In a study of 114 people rejected within eight weeks, 40 percent had measurable depression, and some die of heart attacks or strokes, or commit suicide.

HFI’m a biological anthropologist and senior research fellow at The Kinsey Institute, and I’m also Chief Scientific Advisor to Match.com. It’s the same subject seen from two sides: what happens in the brain when people fall in love, and how people find partners. Match.com hired me in 2005 to help build chemistry.com, and from 2010 I ran its Singles in America survey with the Kinsey Institute.

Pain, purpose and labels

Is a painful mental state like depression or heartbreak a malfunction, or does it serve an evolutionary purpose?

Matthew Hutson

Depression’s symptoms (lost pleasure, rumination, sharper analysis, more REM sleep) form an organised system, so I think it’s designed to focus us on solving the one problem that triggered it. But a past benefit isn’t a present one. Like our sugar cravings, it may be mismatched with modern life, where we’re isolated and our signals go unheeded. Some episodes may come from genetic glitches, and Randolph Nesse notes that being useful doesn’t prove it evolved for that.

Helen Fisher

I’d say love isn’t a malfunction. It’s a basic human drive, and Lucy Brown calls it a survival mechanism as crucial as the craving for water. We share the chemistry with other animals: a female prairie vole’s dopamine activity rises 50 percent when she shows attraction, and dopamine rises with mate attraction in female sheep. It evolved to help animals focus on specific mates and save courtship time and energy. In humans, early-stage love can last far longer than in other species.

Should these states be treated as medical conditions, and how do official labels shape the way society responds?

Matthew Hutson

Hagen compares antidepressants to painkillers, saying it would be unethical to treat a broken ankle with Percocet and no cast, because the underlying conflict stays unresolved. He regrets that the DSM dropped the exception for life circumstances, even bereavement. Psychiatry hasn’t embraced these models. J. Anderson Thomson says it still sees depression purely as illness, and insurance limits push psychiatrists toward prescriptions. Hagen himself says we aren’t ready to apply these theories clinically.

Helen Fisher

Many professionals define addiction as pathological, so because love is often positive, researchers resist classifying it as one. But love addiction is as real as any other in its behaviour patterns and brain mechanisms, and it brings intense craving and anxiety even when it isn’t harmful. Substance and behavioural addictions activate many of the same reward pathways as love. Unlike other addictions, almost everyone experiences some form of it, so I think it should be treated as an addiction, label or not.
